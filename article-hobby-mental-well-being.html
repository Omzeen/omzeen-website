How Starting a Hobby Can Impact Your Mental Well-Being

Life can sometimes feel like a never-ending list of things you need to do. Wake up, study or work, answer messages, meet deadlines, complete responsibilities, and do it all over. Somewhere between all these tasks, we often forget to do something simply because we enjoy it.

That is where hobbies can make a surprisingly meaningful difference.

6 min read 2026 Well-Being

A hobby does not have to be impressive, productive, or something you are naturally good at. It can be as simple as reading a few pages of a book, crocheting a small flower or leaf, tending to a plant, sketching or colouring in a notebook, listening to music, baking something, or taking photographs of things that catch your eye.

Here are some ways hobbies may help support your well-being when you make time for them.

1. They Can Reduce the Body's Stress Response

Painting can give the brain a break from everyday pressure, while the creative process may help reduce the body's physiological response to stress.

In one study of 39 healthy adults, 45 minutes of artmaking produced a statistically significant reduction in cortisol, a hormone involved in the body's stress response. Participants also described the activity as relaxing and enjoyable.

These findings suggest that creative activities may be useful as one part of a broader approach to stress management. However, a hobby should not be viewed as a replacement for professional care when someone is experiencing significant or persistent mental health difficulties.

Try this: Set aside 20 to 30 minutes for a low-pressure creative activity. The goal is not to produce something perfect; the goal is simply to spend some uninterrupted time doing something you enjoy.

2. They Encourage Focused Attention

Hobbies such as crocheting, knitting, drawing, and painting require attention to patterns, movements, colours, shapes, counting, and other small details.

This gives your attention a specific task instead of constantly switching between thoughts and distractions. When you concentrate on a crochet pattern or carefully paint a small section of a picture, your attention stays anchored to one task, giving your mind a chance to step away from constant distractions.

3. They Can Create a "Flow" Experience

Have you ever started painting or crocheting and suddenly realised that an hour had passed?

That experience is often described as flow: being deeply absorbed in an activity. Research on artmaking has reported participants describing feelings of becoming absorbed or "losing themselves" in the creative process.

Creative hobbies can create moments of deep absorption, where you become so involved in what you are doing that outside distractions temporarily fade into the background.

4. Completing Something Creates a Sense of Achievement

A hobby gives you visible evidence of progress. A blank canvas becomes a painting; a ball of yarn becomes a scarf or flower.

Research on needlecraft—including crocheting, knitting, embroidery, and related activities—has identified achievement, satisfaction, purpose, and a sense of value as recurring themes associated with well-being.

A large 2024 study of more than 7,000 adults also found that engagement in arts and crafts predicted greater life satisfaction, happiness, and a sense that life is worthwhile.

Finishing even a small creative project gives you a tangible reminder that your time and effort can turn into something meaningful, which can in turn motivate you to do more.

5. They Provide an Enjoyable Break From Routine

Hobbies are different from responsibilities because they are activities we choose for enjoyment. That distinction matters.

A 2025 review of research on hobbies found associations between hobby participation and relaxation, pleasure, lower stress, and better overall life satisfaction.

Unlike work or study, a hobby gives you time that does not have to be productive—it can simply be enjoyable.

Read the research review on PubMed

6. They Can Support Overall Psychological Well-Being

The evidence is not limited to one craft. A 2025 systematic review of 19 studies on craft-based interventions found short-term improvements across outcomes including anxiety, stress, mood, well-being, self-esteem, life satisfaction, and quality of life.

The researchers also noted that study quality varied and that stronger research is needed to determine how long these benefits last.

Regularly making time for an enjoyable hobby can therefore become one small part of a lifestyle that supports emotional well-being.

A useful perspective: The goal of a hobby does not have to be productivity. Enjoyment, attention, creativity, and the simple experience of doing something by choice can all be meaningful.

Give Yourself Permission to Enjoy the Process

In a world that constantly asks us to be productive, hobbies remind us that our time does not always have to produce something valuable to be worthwhile.

You can make mistakes. You can change hobbies halfway through. You can simply enjoy the process.

So, if you have been waiting for the "perfect" hobby, perhaps start with something simple.

  • Pick up that book.
  • Buy that ball of yarn.
  • Plant that little seed.
  • Draw something silly.
  • Take that photograph.
  • Write that thought.

You do not have to be good at it. You just need to begin.

Conclusion

Sometimes, taking care of your mental well-being can start with something as simple as making time for something that makes you feel like yourself.

A hobby is not a cure for mental health conditions, but it can be one accessible and enjoyable part of a healthy routine. Choose something that feels interesting rather than something that feels like another obligation.
